Grandpa was already up when Injustice came downstairs.

He was sitting at the kitchen table with a cup of coffee he hadn't touched, both hands wrapped around it like he needed something to hold. He looked up when Injustice came in, and for a moment neither of them said anything. The house felt different without her in it. Quieter in the wrong way.

"You eating before you go?" Grandpa asked.

"I'll get something at school."

Grandpa nodded slowly and looked back down at his coffee.

Injustice grabbed his bag.

The school hallways felt the same as they always did. That was almost the strangest part. Lockers slamming, the same conversations, the same noise. Nobody here knew that his grandmother was in critical care. Nobody knew about the lawyer or the bodyguard or the envelope. It was just a Tuesday to everyone else.

He had barely made it to his second period when one of the office aides appeared in the doorway and handed the teacher a slip of paper.

The teacher read it, looked up, and found Injustice's face.

"They need you in the office."

---

The principal's name was Mr. Calloway. He was a compact, carefully dressed man who had a habit of folding his hands on his desk whenever he was about to say something he had already decided on before the conversation began. He gestured to the chair across from him as Injustice came in, and Injustice sat.

"I heard about your grandmother," Calloway said, and his voice carried the practiced weight of a man who had delivered condolences often enough that they no longer cost him anything. "I'm very sorry. That's a terrible thing."

"Thank you."

Calloway unfolded his hands and refolded them. "I also want to say — and I mean this sincerely — that this school takes the wellbeing of its students very seriously." He paused. "Which is why I want to gently encourage you, during what I know is an incredibly difficult time, to be careful about the kinds of stories that can start circulating. Grief can make things feel connected that aren't. It can make people want answers in places where there aren't any clean ones."

Injustice looked at him.

"I'm not sure what you mean," he said.

Calloway held his gaze for exactly one second too long before smiling. "I just mean that you have a lot on your plate right now. Focus on your family. Focus on your studies. We're all rooting for your grandmother's recovery." He stood, signaling that the meeting was over. "My door is always open."

Injustice stood, picked up his bag, and walked out without another word.

The door clicked shut behind him.

---

After school he went to the hospital.

The hallways smelled like antiseptic and recycled air, the kind of place where time moves differently — slower and more urgent at the same time. He asked at the front desk for Doctor Jones and was told, politely, that Doctor Jones was unavailable.

A woman appeared instead. She introduced herself as Ms. Farrell, Patient Services Administrator, and guided him to a small side room with two chairs and a box of tissues on a table that nobody had used. She sat across from him and folded a manila folder onto her lap.

"We wanted to check in with you about your grandmother's care plan going forward," she began. "As you know, her condition requires ongoing monitoring, and there are some decisions that will need to be made soon regarding her treatment options." She opened the folder and produced a form. "One thing we do encourage in situations like this is for families to consolidate their focus — let us handle the medical side, and try to avoid outside stressors that might complicate recovery."

She slid the form across to him. It was a release of some kind, dense with language he would need time to actually read.

"There's no rush on this today," she said, in a tone that suggested there was. "But sooner is generally better for everyone. For her especially."

Injustice looked at the form and did not touch it.

"Where is Doctor Jones?" he asked.

A small pause. "Doctor Jones is attending to other patients. I'm the right person to speak with about the administrative side of things." She smiled. "We really do want what's best for your grandmother."

He left the form on the table when he stood up.

---

The police station was a twenty minute bus ride from the hospital. He stood at the front desk and gave his name and explained that he was following up on the hit and run investigation — his grandmother, the incident two days ago, the case number he had written in his phone.

The officer at the desk typed something, waited, typed something else.

"That case is still open," the officer said, not looking up.

"Has there been any progress?"

"Investigations take time."

"It's been two days. There was a witness. There's a whole street that —"

"Sir." The officer looked up now, patient and immovable. "These things take time. We have a lot of cases. Someone will be in touch when there's something to share."

"Can I speak to whoever is assigned to it?"

"They're not available right now."

"Can I leave my number?"

"We already have your number on file."

Injustice stood there for a moment. The officer had already looked back down at his screen.

There was nothing left to say. He turned and walked back out through the glass doors into the late afternoon air.

---

He was sitting on the low wall outside the station, not quite ready to get back on the bus, when he heard footsteps stop nearby.

"Hey."

He looked up. It was Dillon.

He looked like he hadn't slept well either. He had his hands in his jacket pockets and he stood a few feet away like he wasn't sure if he was welcome.

"I heard about your grandma," Dillon said. "Someone told me this morning." He paused. "I just wanted to — I don't know. I just wanted to say I'm sorry. What happened wasn't —" He stopped again, seemed to reconsider his words. "It wasn't right."

Injustice looked at him for a moment.

"You know it was him," Injustice said. It wasn't a question.

Dillon didn't answer right away. Then, quietly: "Yeah."

Neither of them said anything for a while after that. The street moved around them — buses, pedestrians, the city doing what the city does regardless.

Eventually Dillon sat down on the wall a few feet away, and they just sat there together for a little while, two people who didn't have anywhere better to be.

---

He got home after dark.

Grandpa had left the porch light on.

The business card from Breakers Inc was still on the kitchen table where he had left it, next to the unopened bakery box. He stood in the kitchen for a long moment, looking at both of them.

Then he went to check on Grandpa, turned off the lights, and went to bed.
