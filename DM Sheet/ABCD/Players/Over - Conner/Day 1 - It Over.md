Friday came in grey and unremarkable. Over had slept through his first alarm and woken up to the second one with that immediate, stomach-dropping inventory that had become his morning ritual — _internship report, Mr. Chen, no response yet, rent, school, shift tonight_ — all of it loading in before he'd even sat up.

He was halfway through getting dressed when his phone buzzed with a text.

_you eat yet_

He hadn't. He typed back _no_ and the reply came in under ten seconds.

_meet me at the place. 20 min_

---

Marcus was already there when Over arrived, sitting at the corner table of the small noodle shop two blocks from campus like he owned it, which he kind of acted like he did everywhere he went. He had a way of occupying space that Over had never quite been able to name — not loud, not pushy, just completely unbothered, like the concept of things going wrong had simply never occurred to him as a real possibility.

He was a year ahead of Over. Business major too, though he wore it loosely, the way he wore most things — jacket half-zipped, posture relaxed, already halfway through a bowl he'd ordered without waiting.

"You look terrible," Marcus said, by way of greeting.

"Thanks," Over said, and sat down.

Marcus had already ordered for him. That was a thing he did — not asking, just knowing, which should have been annoying but wasn't, because he was always right, and because there were months in Over's first year when those meals had been the most reliable thing on his schedule. Over had never asked him to. Marcus had never made a thing of it. It was just quietly, consistently there, the same way Marcus had been when he'd forwarded Over the internship listing with a single message: _apply. you'll get it._ No explanation of how he'd known about it, no fanfare when Over did get it. Just a thumbs up emoji and then nothing more about it.

Over had always figured Marcus was just lucky. The kind of person things worked out for, not because he was careless but because the universe seemed to have agreed, somewhere along the way, to cooperate with him.

They talked through the meal the way they usually did — easy, unhurried, Marcus asking questions about the Chen situation with the mild curiosity of someone watching an interesting weather pattern from inside a warm building. Over told him about the redo, about the silence since, about the way Mr. Chen had looked at him when he handed it in.

"He didn't say anything?" Marcus asked.

"Nothing."

Marcus nodded slowly, turning his chopsticks between his fingers. "That's either good or really bad."

"Yeah." Over stared at his bowl. "I know."

There was a beat of comfortable quiet. Outside, the street was starting to fill up with the usual Friday morning foot traffic — students, commuters, a guy arguing loudly with someone on speakerphone. Normal city sounds. Over let himself sit in it for a moment without thinking about anything.

Then Marcus leaned back in his chair and looked at him with that easy, half-lidded expression he got sometimes, like something had just occurred to him.

"Hey," he said. "Wish me luck today."

Over looked up. "What?"

"I've got a thing this afternoon. Just — wish me luck."

It was such a simple, throwaway request. Marcus said it the way you'd ask someone to pass the salt. Over didn't think about it. Why would he?

"Good luck," Over said.

Marcus smiled — just slightly, just briefly — and looked back down at his bowl.

---

Over's phone buzzed on the walk back.

He almost didn't check it. He was already mentally running through the afternoon — school, then the call center at six, and somewhere in between he needed to respond to a discussion board post he'd been putting off since Wednesday. He pulled out his phone expecting a reminder notification or a message from his group chat.

It was an letter. The subject line read:

**NOTICE OF LOAN DISBURSEMENT DISCREPANCY – ACTION REQUIRED**

He stopped walking. Someone behind him muttered something and stepped around him.

Over read it once, quickly, the way you read something when you're hoping you misunderstood it. Then he read it again, slowly.

The short version: there had been a miscalculation in his student loan disbursement from the previous academic year. An overpayment had been made to his account. The figure had gone unaddressed. With administrative fees and accrued interest, the total amount now owed back to the loan servicer was—

Over's chest did something unpleasant.

He stood on the sidewalk in the grey Friday morning, people moving around him, and stared at a number on his phone screen that did not belong anywhere near his bank account balance.

At the bottom of the email, in clean, indifferent font: _Failure to respond within 30 days may result in legal escalation.
